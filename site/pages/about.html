Our purpose

NAGP is being created to serve as a professional home for Gestalt practitioners across North America. We want to offer community, continuing education, professional standards, and a national voice for the field.

At the first national gathering, on April 5, 2025, the people present drafted this initial purpose:

“To enliven and expand a Gestalt theory and practice in the United States through shared leadership and participation in research, mentoring, and advocacy.”

The Founding Council is now refining NAGP's mission and values. We will share the updated wording here.

The Covenant of Community

The Covenant of Community will describe how we choose to be together as a community: what we value, and what we promise one another.

Please send the text of the Covenant of Community. Until then, this section shows an outline of what we expect it to cover. Please correct the outline if it is wrong.

We expect the Covenant to cover:

  • The values we share as a Gestalt community
  • How we treat one another, including when we disagree
  • How we make decisions together and share leadership
  • How we welcome people from every training background, profession and culture
  • How we repair trust when something goes wrong
  • Our commitments to clients, students and the public

Our Community Guidelines put these values into practice for the community board, announcements and meetings.

The Gestalt framework

Gestalt therapy is a humanistic, experiential approach to psychotherapy. It pays close attention to what is happening right now, in the person and between people.

Gestalt is a German word for a form or a meaningful whole. The approach drew on psychoanalysis, Gestalt psychology, the holistic ideas of the neurologist Kurt Goldstein, and existential and phenomenological philosophy. It was developed in the 1940s and 1950s by Frederick (Fritz) Perls, Laura Perls and Paul Goodman, working with others. The founding book, Gestalt Therapy: Excitement and Growth in the Human Personality, by Frederick Perls, Ralph Hefferline and Paul Goodman, was published in 1951.

A few ideas are at the heart of the Gestalt approach:

Awareness

Awareness is noticing what you sense, feel, think and do, as it happens. In Gestalt work, change grows out of fuller awareness of what is, rather than from trying to become someone you are not. Arnold Beisser called this “the paradoxical theory of change” in 1970.

The here and now

Gestalt work attends to present experience. Past events and future worries matter, and we meet them as they live in us today: in the body, in feelings, and in how we relate to others.

Contact

Contact is how we meet the world and other people. Gestalt practitioners pay attention to how a person reaches out, holds back, or loses touch, and to the ways of coping that once helped but now get in the way.

The field

A person cannot be understood apart from their surroundings. Field theory, drawn in part from the psychologist Kurt Lewin, looks at the whole situation: relationships, family, culture, community and the wider world.

Dialogue and the relationship

The relationship between practitioner and client is at the center of the work. Drawing on the philosopher Martin Buber, Gestalt practitioners aim to meet people with presence, respect and honesty, rather than as an expert who fixes them.

Experiment

Instead of only talking about a difficulty, client and practitioner may try something new together in the session. This might be speaking to an empty chair, paying attention to breath or posture, or saying something in a different way. The aim is to discover, not to perform.

These ideas are used well beyond the therapy room: in coaching, in organizations and groups, in education, and in community life.

Who NAGP is for

NAGP welcomes everyone who puts Gestalt theory into practice, and everyone who values it.

  • Clinicians: psychotherapists, counselors, social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists and other mental health professionals.
  • Coaches who work with individuals, leaders and teams.
  • Organizational development consultants who bring a Gestalt view to groups, teams and organizations.
  • Academics and trainers who teach, write about and research Gestalt theory and practice.
  • Students and trainees in Gestalt training programs or graduate study.
  • Friends of Gestalt: people who are not practicing professionals but who value the Gestalt approach and want to belong.

Whether you are a seasoned Gestalt therapist or just beginning your training, you belong here.
